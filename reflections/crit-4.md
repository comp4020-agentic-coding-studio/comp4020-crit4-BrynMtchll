# Reflection
Breakthrough: I built a theremin first, tested it, and observed that continuous mouse-driven glissando lost expressiveness after about ten seconds of use. I discarded this version before committing it, so no diff records the change. The current design, the drawn spectrogram surface, the chord-locked pitch bands, and the 59-test theory module, followed from that decision. The key point is that the decision was based on direct observation rather than any automated check.

What's changed: Over the course of this project, three separate checks or diagnoses that appeared correct were later found to be wrong: a modality test that passed with only one input type present, an erasure bug whose output fell below the threshold at which it became visible, and a drum-bleed issue that was initially attributed to timbre rather than spatial overlap between bands. In each case, the error was identified only after taking a direct measurement (an RMS reading, a pixel count, or a corrected assertion) rather than accepting a plausible-sounding explanation. Based on this, I now apply a general rule: any property of the system that can be verified through a test or measurement should be, and manual judgment (by ear or by eye) should be reserved for properties that cannot be checked this way, such as latency or perceived playability. A secondary observation was that this applies equally to checks I wrote myself; one of the incorrect checks was my own, not the agent's. Going forward, I intend to treat correcting a faulty check as an equally important outcome to correcting the underlying bug it failed to catch.
